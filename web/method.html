The short version

When someone's heart stops, a bystander with an automated external defibrillator (AED) can restart it before the ambulance arrives. Cities publish where their AEDs are. They do not publish when you can actually get to one: most sit inside buildings that lock at night, at weekends and over the summer.

After Hours gives each of New York's {{nyc:findings.locations|int}} registered AED locations a weekly timetable, draws the area within a two-minute walk of it on real streets, and replays {{nyc:findings.calls|int}} cardiac-arrest 911 calls from 2025 against those timetables. The result: {{nyc:findings.coverageLost.lostConservative|pct}} of the coverage a normal AED map shows was behind a locked door at the hour the calls came in.

It then names the AEDs to move first: school defibrillators that, in a weatherproof cabinet on the street wall, would have been within reach of the most of those calls.

Walking areas

Research on AED coverage usually draws a 100 m circle. A circle walks through buildings and across rivers. Amazon Location Service (Routes, CalculateIsolines, travel mode Pedestrian) drew the area a person can walk in two minutes from each of the {{nyc:findings.locations|int}} locations, along real streets.

Two minutes there and two minutes back means the first shock comes about four minutes after the collapse, plus the time the AED takes to check the rhythm. Each minute without CPR and defibrillation costs roughly 7 to 10% of the chance of survival.

Coverage

We laid a 50 m grid over the city ({{nyc:cells|int}} squares) and, for every hour of a school-year week and a summer week, asked of each square: is it inside the walking area of an AED that is open now?

Calls are only published by ZIP code, so each ZIP's calls at a given hour are spread evenly over its squares. That gives two numbers we can compare:

  • What a time-blind map shows: {{nyc:calls.byVariant.18.timeBlindCoverage|pct1}} of call-weighted area is within two minutes of some AED.
  • What was open when the calls came: {{nyc:calls.byVariant.18.actualCoverage|pct1}}. If every venue of unknown hours were open around the clock, it would still be only {{nyc:calls.byVariant.18.unknownCoverage|pct1}} higher.

Both numbers are small, because two minutes on foot is a small area and New York is large. The point is the gap between them: {{nyc:findings.coverageLost.lostConservative|pct}} of the coverage disappears once you ask whether the door was open.

The ranked list credits each grid square to its nearest school AED and adds up the 2025 calls that fell there while every AED in reach was locked.

Does the ranking beat simpler choices? We scored every way of picking schools with one measure: the expected 2025 cardiac-arrest calls a year inside the walk of at least one chosen school at hours when no AED in reach was open, counting a street square once however many chosen schools reach it. Calls are only known by ZIP code, so these are modelled call counts, not people reached or lives saved.

Knowing the hours is what earns the gain: a map that ignores them picks schools that are near calls but whose calls mostly come in while the school is open. The greedy method, which re-scores every school after each pick, does slightly better; we keep the simpler ranking because each school's figure is then its own share, which a one-page brief can state. The map shows that sum ({{nyc:rankingEval.methods.3.afterHoursCredited}} for the top 100) as "at least 84".

Estimating hours. Before using Amazon Bedrock (Claude Haiku) on venues with no published hours, we tested it blind on {{nyc:aiGrade.venuesWithKnownHours|int}} venues whose hours Amazon Location Service does publish.

A wrong "open" sends someone to a locked door, so AI estimates stay out of the headline numbers. Switch them on in the map to see how much they would change.

Hearing both sides. For any school on the list, two agents argue whether to move its AED outdoors, and a third gives a verdict. They receive eight numbered lines of evidence from the data and must cite them. Sentences that state a number without a citation are counted and shown under every debate.

Reading questions. A question typed into the map is read by Amazon Bedrock into a place, a day or date, an hour, and two flags: is the place inside New York City, and is someone collapsing right now. It cannot answer. Code turns the day into the hour of the week, Amazon Location Service finds the place, and the map's own engine gives the answer. A landmark only counts if the result carries the name asked for, so a miss says "couldn't find" instead of showing a different place. We test it with the clock fixed at {{nyc:evals.ask.clock}}.

Registry watch

Every night Amazon EventBridge Scheduler starts a job that copies New York's AED registry into an Apache Iceberg table on Amazon S3, using Amazon Athena. Iceberg keeps every version, so the job can compare tonight with the last night and publish what changed.

The pipeline

  1. Open dataNYC Open Data, Géo'DAE, Overture Maps on the Registry of Open Data on AWS
  2. HoursSchool calendar, Amazon Location Places, Amazon Bedrock
  3. Walking areasAmazon Location Routes isolines
  4. Coverage50 m grid × 336 hours, call-weighted
  5. StoreAmazon S3, Apache Iceberg via Amazon Athena
  6. ServeAmazon CloudFront, Amazon API Gateway, AWS Lambda, MCP

To reuse it for another city: export your AED list with an opening_hours column, drop it on the Your data page or send it to POST /api/v1/analyze. To run the whole thing yourself: npm run pipeline, then cdk deploy.

Limits, measured

  • Calls are by ZIP code, not address. We spread each ZIP's calls evenly over its streets. The ranking is a pointer for a site visit, not a verdict.
  • Most cardiac arrests happen at home. The calls include them. A street-wall AED a short walk away is still the nearest one for a home.
  • Not every call is a cardiac arrest. We use the dispatcher's final call type. Of the 21,287 calls in 2025 first coded as cardiac arrest, 1 in 5 were later recoded as something else, and we left those out.
  • {{nyc:hoursSources.ai|int}} locations have estimated hours and {{nyc:hoursSources.unknown|int}} have none. Both are excluded from the headline numbers. Counting every one as open around the clock still leaves {{nyc:findings.coverageLost.lostIfUnknownWereOpen|pct}} of coverage locked.
  • School hours are an assumption. Some schools have evening programmes or security staff. The map lets you set the closing time at 3, 6 or 9 PM; at 9 PM, {{nyc:findings.coverageLostSchools9pm.lostConservative|pct}} of coverage is still lost.
  • An open building is not always an open AED. Some sit behind a desk or in a locked office. Real access is lower than shown.
  • The registry is incomplete. {{nyc:registry.registryRows|int}} rows describe {{nyc:findings.locations|int}} locations; many workplaces never register their AEDs.
